Overview: Three Complementary Approaches

This calculator implements three independent interpretive frameworks for acid-base disorders. Each uses different variables and equations but should converge on similar management recommendations:

  • Hughes & Brain: Bedside-friendly 8-step flowchart emphasising clinical pattern recognition and actionable drivers
  • Stewart: Physicochemical approach using strong ion difference (SID), weak acid effects (Atot), and respiratory component
  • Traditional Boston: Anion gap method with Delta ratio and differential diagnosis mnemonics
  • Figge-Fencl: Detects unmeasured ions by comparing predicted vs. measured pH

Hughes & Brain Approach

Reference: Hughes R & Brain MJ. "A simplified bedside approach to acid-base: fluid physiology utilising classical and physicochemical approaches." Anaesthesia and Intensive Care Medicine 14:10 (2013): 445–452.

8-Step Flowchart

  1. pH Status: Acidaemia (<7.35) vs. Normal (7.35-7.45) vs. Alkalaemia (>7.45)
  2. pCO2 Assessment: Hypoventilation (>45) vs. Normal (35-45) vs. Hyperventilation (<35). Determine if compensatory or primary pathology.
  3. BEecf (Base Excess in extracellular fluid): Metabolic component
    BEecf = −0.93 × (24 − HCO3) − 16 × (7.40 − pH)
    Normal: −3 to +3 mEq/L
  4. Anion Gap (raw): AG = Na − (Cl + HCO3) Normal: 8–16
  5. Anion Gap (corrected for albumin):
    AG_corrected = AG + (40 − Albumin)/4
    Accounts for unmeasured negative charge from hypoalbuminaemia
  6. Lactate: 0.5–2.2 mmol/L normal. High lactate suggests tissue hypoperfusion/hypoxia
  7. Electrolytes (Na, K, Cl):
    • Na: Low Na acidifies (↓SID); high Na alkalinises (↑SID)
    • Corrected Cl: Cl(corrected) = Measured Cl × (140 / Na) — Reveals relative hyperchloraemia even if measured Cl borderline
    • K context-dependent: In acidaemia, low K = severe total-body depletion (will drop further as pH corrects); high K = expected, will drop. In alkalaemia, low K is critical arrhythmia risk.
  8. Ancillary (Creatinine, Glucose, Osmolality): Assess renal function, metabolic stress, unmeasured osmoles

Clinical Drivers & SID-Based Corrections

The Hughes & Brain tab identifies critical derangements and uses SID-based calculations for transparent, actionable interventions:

  • Hyperkalaemia (K >6): Calcium gluconate, insulin + dextrose, salbutamol, dialysis
  • Hypokalaemia (K <3.5 in acidaemia): Paradoxical depletion signal—KPO4 preferred if concurrent hyperchloraemia; expect further drop as pH corrects
  • Hypoalbuminaemia: Contributes to acidaemia via low Atot, but albumin infusion is inefficient for pH correction. Focus on SID-based electrolyte management instead.
  • Elevated lactate: Aggressive resuscitation, vasopressors, source control. Can partially correct pH via NaHCO3 SID correction while treating perfusion.
  • Severe metabolic acidosis (hyperchloremic, lactate-driven, DKA): Calculate Na deficit needed for target pH, then: NaHCO3 (mL of 8.4%) = Na deficit × 0.6 × 80 / 1. Give HALF initially, recheck ABG in 30 min, reassess before giving remainder (half-correction strategy).
  • Hyperchloraemia (Cl >110 or corrected Cl >110): Switch to balanced fluids (Hartmann's, Plasmalyte). Use NaHCO3 to raise Na and improve SID.

Ventilation Management

Compensatory Hyperventilation (pCO2 <30, pH <7.35): Do NOT reduce ventilation. Expect minute ventilation to decrease by ~37–45% as metabolic acidosis is treated. If mechanically ventilated, actively reduce RR/tidal volume as acidosis improves to avoid iatrogenic respiratory alkalosis.

Primary Hyperventilation (pCO2 <30, pH ≥7.35): This IS the problem. If spontaneous, address driver (anxiety, pain, fever). If mechanical, reduce minute ventilation to target pCO2 35–40.

Compensatory Hypoventilation (pCO2 >55, pH >7.45): Do NOT over-ventilate. Let it be. If mechanical, do not increase RR/tidal volume until alkalosis is fully treated.

Primary Hypoventilation (pCO2 >55, pH <7.45): This IS inadequate ventilation. Increase minute ventilation to target pCO2 35–45. If mechanical, fix the settings.

Stewart Approach

Reference: Stewart PA. "Modern quantitative acid-base chemistry." Canadian Journal of Physiology and Pharmacology 61 (1983): 1444–1461.

Three Independent Variables

Stewart's principle: pH is determined by three and only three independent variables. Changing any one affects pH predictably:

1. Strong Ion Difference (SID)

SID = (Na + K + 2×iCa) − (Cl + Lactate)
  • Normal: 38–42 mEq/L
  • Low SID (↓Na, ↓K, ↑Cl, ↑Lactate) → Acidifying
  • High SID (↑Na, ↑K, ↓Cl, ↓Lactate) → Alkalinising

2. Weak Acid Effect (Atot)

Atot = (Albumin/10) × (0.123 × pH − 0.631) + (PO4/10) × (0.309 × pH − 0.469)
  • Normal: ~18 mEq/L
  • Low Atot (hypoalbuminaemia, low PO4) → Acidifying effect
  • CLINICAL NOTE: Atot is difficult to correct meaningfully (albumin is hard to increment). Focus on SID correction instead.

3. Respiratory Component (pCO2)

Predicted pH = 7.4 + 0.16×(SID−38) + 0.04×(Atot−18) − 0.084×((pCO2−40)/7.5)
  • ↑pCO2 (>45) → Acidifying
  • ↓pCO2 (<35) → Alkalinising

Clinically Relevant Levers

Only TWO variables are practically useful for correction:

  • SID Management: Adjust Na, K, Cl via fluids (balanced vs. NaCl), supplementation, or osmotic therapy
  • Ventilation: Adjust minute ventilation (RR × tidal volume) to target pCO2

Atot is supporting context but NOT a primary intervention target.

Traditional Boston Approach

Reference: Goldberg M, Green SB, Moss ML, et al. "Computer-based instruction and diagnosis of acid-base disorders." JAMA 223 (1973): 269–275.

5-Step Method

  1. Determine pH status: Acidaemia vs. Alkalaemia vs. Normal
  2. Assess respiratory response (Winter's Formula): For acidaemia only
    Expected pCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3 + 8 ± 2
    • If measured pCO2
    • If measured pCO2 >expected + 2: Concurrent respiratory acidosis
    • If within ±2: Appropriate compensation
  3. Calculate Anion Gap (uncorrected): AG = Na − (Cl + HCO3) Normal: 8–16
  4. Correct AG for albumin:
    AG_corrected = AG + (40 − Albumin)/4
  5. If HAGMA (AG >16), calculate Delta Ratio:
    Δ Ratio = ΔAG / ΔHCO3 = (AG − 12) / (24 − HCO3)
    • >2: Concurrent metabolic alkalosis
      • <1: Concurrent normal AG metabolic acidosis (mixed picture)
    • 1–2: Pure high AG metabolic acidosis

Differential Diagnosis Mnemonics

GOLDMARK (High AG Metabolic Acidosis):

  • Glycols (ethylene glycol, propylene glycol)
  • Oxoproline (acetaminophen toxicity)
  • L-lactate (hyperlactataemia)
  • D-lactate (short bowel syndrome)
  • Methanol
  • Aspirin (salicylate toxicity)
  • Renal failure
  • Ketoacidosis (DKA, alcoholic ketoacidosis)

USEDCARS (Normal AG Metabolic Acidosis):

  • Ureterosigmoidostomy (chloride loss in urine)
  • Saline administration (hyperchloraemic acidosis)
  • Endocrine (Addison's disease, hypoaldosteronism)
  • Diarrhoea (bicarbonate loss, chloride retention)
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide)
  • Ammonium chloride (exogenous)
  • RTA (renal tubular acidosis: Types 1, 2, 4)
  • Spironolactone (Type 4 RTA via hyperkalemia)

Osmolar Gap (if measured osmolality available)

Calculated Osmolality = 2×Na + (Glucose/18) + (Urea/2.8) + 10
Osmolar Gap = Measured Osmolality − Calculated Osmolality
  • Explained osmolar gap: Lactate contributes ~1 mOsm per 4 mmol/L; other unmeasured ions (PO4, Mg) ~2 mOsm
  • Residual gap = Total gap − Explained gap
  • Residual <5 mOsm/kg: Normal. No concern for toxic alcohol.
  • Residual 5–15 mOsm/kg: ⚠️ Context-dependent. In sepsis with high lactate = expected and acceptable. In altered mental status + hypotension = concerning for toxic alcohol.
  • Residual >15 mOsm/kg: 🔴 Highly suspicious for toxic alcohol (methanol, ethylene glycol, isopropanol). If ethylene glycol suspected: Fomepizole 15 mg/kg IV loading, then 10 mg/kg q12h IV.

Figge-Fencl Approach

Reference: Figge J, Mydosh T, Fencl V. "Serum electrolytes and acid-base status in critically ill patients." American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine 150 (1994): 140–147.

Unmeasured Ion Detection

Compares the predicted pH from Stewart equation against measured pH to detect unmeasured ions:

Predicted pH = 7.4 + 0.16×(SID−38) + 0.04×(Atot−18) − 0.084×((pCO2−40)/7.5)
  • If measured pH ≈ predicted pH: Measured ions (Na, K, Cl, Lactate) fully explain the acid-base status
  • If measured pH significantly different from predicted: Unmeasured ions present
    • Unmeasured cations: Calcium, magnesium, unidentified cations
    • Unmeasured anions: Phosphate, sulfate, organic acids

Clinical Significance

  • Hypermagnesaemia (from laxatives, renal failure)
  • Hypercalcaemia (parathyroid disease, malignancy, vitamin D toxicity)
  • Severe phosphate derangements (renal failure)
  • Calciphylaxis or other unmeasured organic acid accumulation

Key Teaching Principles

  • Three frameworks, one bedside: Hughes & Brain, Stewart, and Traditional Boston each provide independent but complementary interpretations. They should converge on similar management.
  • Corrected chloride (Cl × 140/Na) reveals hidden patterns: A borderline measured Cl (108) in context of low Na (135) shows corrected Cl = 112, revealing hyperchloraemic pattern that raw Cl would miss. Always interpret Cl relative to Na.
  • K paradox in acidaemia: Low K in acidaemia = severe total-body depletion (because H+ shifts push K out of cells → K into serum, then excreted → net loss). As pH corrects, K will drop further. Use KPO4 if concurrent hyperchloraemia.
  • SID-based NaHCO3 dosing is transparent: Calculate Na deficit → bicarb mL needed (Na deficit × 0.6 × 80 / 1). But ALWAYS use half-correction: give HALF initially, recheck ABG in 30 min, reassess before giving remainder. Avoids hypernatraemia, alkalosis, K crash.
  • SID is the main lever in Stewart: Atot is theoretically important but practically hard to move meaningfully (albumin infusion is inefficient for pH). Focus on correcting strong ions (Na, Cl, K, Lactate).
  • Hyperchloraemia matters: High Cl or corrected Cl with normal AG indicates NAGMA, not HAGMA. Managed via balanced fluids and SID correction (NaHCO3), not bicarb alone.
  • Compensatory ventilation is context-sensitive: Hyperventilation in acidaemia is APPROPRIATE—do NOT reduce ventilation spontaneously; if mechanical, you MUST reduce MV as acidosis improves or risk iatrogenic alkalosis. Hypoventilation in alkalaemia is APPROPRIATE—do NOT over-ventilate; if mechanical, hold RR until alkalosis resolved.
  • Osmolar gap requires decomposition: High osmolar gap ≠ automatic toxic alcohol. Lactate contributes ~1 mOsm per 4 mmol/L. Subtract lactate + unmeasured ions from total gap. Only the RESIDUAL gap >15 is truly concerning for toxic alcohol. In sepsis with lactate=10 and gap=+15? That's expected, not alarming.
  • Context-specific corrections: The same electrolyte abnormality has different management depending on pH state, respiratory status, and other drivers. A K of 5.8 in acidaemia (expected, will drop) is managed differently than K=5.8 in alkalaemia (urgent, need K removal).
